Facilitator Guide

Psych Safety Challenge

A meeting room game for leadership teams. 8 rounds. 2–4 teams. ~45 minutes.

What This Game Is

The Psych Safety Challenge is a Family Feud-style game where teams compete to identify what employees actually want to hear in real workplace moments. The twist: the "obvious" answer is often wrong — and the most experienced managers in the room are most likely to get it wrong.

Each round presents a scenario. Teams answer verbally. The host judges whether the answer is close enough to one of the 5 ranked options and awards points. The ranking reflects employee preferences based on psychological safety research.

Setup (5 minutes)

1
Open the game on a laptop Share your screen to a TV or projector. The game is designed for a single facilitator-controlled screen — no phones needed.
2
Divide into teams 2–4 teams work well. 3–6 people per team is ideal. Name your teams on the setup screen.
3
Brief the room "We're going to look at real workplace scenarios. Your job is to figure out what employees actually want to hear — not what sounds good in theory."

How a Round Works

1
Read the scenario out loud Give teams 30–60 seconds to discuss quietly before answering.
2
Take an answer from one team Click their team name at the top to make them active (it highlights).
3
Open the Judge Panel Click "Judge Answer" — only you can see this. It shows all 5 answers with points.
4
Award or strike If their answer is close enough to one on the board, click Award. If not, click Strike. After 2 strikes, the other team gets to steal.
5
Discuss After each reveal, the insight text shows automatically. Use the Facilitator Note button for deeper discussion prompts.
6
Next Round You can move on at any time — you don't need to reveal all 5 answers. Hit "Next Round" when the discussion is rich enough.

Scoring

RankWhat It MeansPoints
#1Most preferred by employees — highest trust response500
#2Strong response — collaborative, supportive400
#3Good but incomplete — validates without acting300
#4Well-intentioned but disempowering200
#5Common management default — lowest trust response100

Points are cumulative across all 8 rounds. The team with the most points at the end wins.

Facilitation Tips

Let experienced managers go first

Senior managers tend to give the most confident wrong answers. Their reactions when the board reveals are the most powerful moments in the room.

Use "Why?" as your main tool

After every reveal, ask: "Why do you think employees ranked that higher than the more direct response?" The discussion is the learning — not the score.

Watch for the "I hear you, but..."

Round 2 (Public Disagreement) consistently surfaces managers who use this phrase. Name it when it happens — it's one of the most insight-generating moments.

Round 8 hits hardest

The Mistake You Made is the most powerful round. Consider saving extra time for discussion here — many managers have never been asked to model public accountability. Let the silence breathe.

You don't have to finish all 8 rounds

If the room is deep in discussion after Round 5, stay there. The game is a vehicle for conversation, not a race.

Round Reference & Answer Key

All answers listed #1 (most preferred by employees) → #5 (least preferred). Points shown. Use this to judge answers off-screen.

Round 1
When Things Go Wrong
Your top performer sent a report with a major error to a client. They come to you looking mortified. We surveyed employees: What do you most want your manager to say first?
#AnswerPts
1"Let's figure out what happened and fix it together."500
2"What do you need from me right now?"400
3"It's okay — everyone makes mistakes."300
4"I'll take care of it."200
5"This can't happen again."100
Watch for: managers who jump to "I'll handle it." Sounds decisive — employees experience it as disempowering.
Round 2
Public Disagreement
A junior employee directly contradicts your recommendation in front of the whole team. We surveyed employees: What response makes them most likely to speak up again?
#AnswerPts
1"That's an interesting angle — tell me more."500
2Pause, genuinely consider their point, then respond.400
3"I appreciate the pushback — let's explore both options."300
4"Let's take this offline."200
5"I hear you, but here's why my approach is right..."100
Watch for: "let's take this offline." Feels diplomatic — signals to the room that public disagreement is unwelcome.
Round 3
The Disengaged Star
Your highest performer has been missing deadlines and seems checked out. In your 1:1 they say "I'm fine." We surveyed employees: What response makes you most likely to open up?
#AnswerPts
1"I've noticed some changes — I'm not calling you out, I'm genuinely worried about you."500
2"You don't have to share anything — but I'm here if that changes."400
3"What would make work feel better right now?"300
4"Is there anything outside work I should know about?"200
5"Okay — let's talk about these missed deadlines then."100
Watch for: pivoting immediately to missed deadlines. The employee hears: "I'm here to manage output, not you."
Round 4
The Bad Idea
In a brainstorm, someone proposes an idea you're 90% sure won't work. We surveyed employees: What response keeps people contributing ideas in the future?
#AnswerPts
1"What problem are you most trying to solve with this?"500
2"Let's put it on the board — what would we need to test it cheaply?"400
3"Interesting — what do others think?"300
4"I like the thinking, but I'm not sure the timing is right."200
5"We tried something like that before — it didn't work."100
Watch for: "we tried that before." The most idea-killing phrase in management — almost always said by the most experienced person in the room.
Round 5
Team Conflict
Two of your best people are in open conflict affecting the whole team. You've pulled them both in. We surveyed employees: What does a manager do that makes this better — not worse?
#AnswerPts
1Listen to each person separately first, then bring them together with clear ground rules.500
2"I'm not here to judge — I need us to figure out how we work together going forward."400
3Name the impact on the team without blaming either person.300
4"I need you both to be professionals and move past this."200
5Identify who's more at fault and address them directly.100
Watch for: jumping straight to the joint meeting. Separate conversations first is the #1 thing managers skip.
Round 6
Delivering Hard Feedback
A solid team member asks directly: "Am I on track for a promotion this cycle?" They're not. We surveyed employees: What do you want most in this moment — even if it's hard to hear?
#AnswerPts
1"Honestly — not this cycle. Here's what I'm seeing and what would change that."500
2"I want to be honest with you — can I share what I'm observing?"400
3"It's too early to say — a lot can happen this cycle."300
4"You're doing great — let's see how the next few months go."200
5"That's really a conversation for the review cycle."100
Watch for: "you're doing great, let's see how the next few months go." Employees will be blindsided at review and lose trust in all future positive feedback.
Round 7
Taking Credit
In an exec presentation, your manager presents your team's work and takes full credit — without naming anyone. We surveyed team members: What does a psychologically safe manager do in this moment?
#AnswerPts
1Proactively name the team members who did the work in the meeting itself.500
2Debrief with the team afterward and acknowledge their contribution explicitly.400
3Send a follow-up email to leadership naming contributors.300
4Nothing — it reflects well on the whole team anyway.200
5It's not appropriate to interrupt the presentation.100
Watch for: "it reflects well on the whole team anyway." Teams notice exactly who gets named and who doesn't. Always.
Round 8
The Mistake You Made
You made a call that set the team back two weeks. Your team knows it. You're in the weekly standup. We surveyed employees: What does your manager say that most restores your trust in them?
#AnswerPts
1"I made the wrong call on this. It cost us two weeks and that's on me — here's what I'm doing differently."500
2"I'd make a different decision with what I know now — and I want to hear what you saw that I missed."400
3"This didn't go the way I expected — let's talk about what we learned."300
4"There were a lot of factors at play — here's the context."200
5"Let's focus on moving forward — what do we need to do this week?"100
This is the most powerful round. Full public ownership, without hedging, is the highest-trust response — and the one most leaders avoid. Let the room sit with that.

The Research Behind It

  • Amy Edmondson (Harvard) — Psychological safety is the #1 predictor of high-performing teams. It's not about being nice; it's about making people feel safe to speak up, take risks, and be honest.
  • Google Project Aristotle — After studying 180 teams, psychological safety was the single most important factor in team effectiveness — above skills, seniority, or structure.
  • Kim Scott (Radical Candor) — The kindest thing a manager can do is tell people the truth early enough to change. Avoiding hard feedback isn't kindness — it's self-protection.
